Munchly Foods makes snacks in Pune. In a distributor's warehouse in Nagpur sit 77 cartons of Masala Chips that the delivery apps will no longer take. This is what happens to them, packet by packet, rupee by rupee, when an AI agent gets to choose their next home.

Munchly Foods · fictional client Batch MF-2409-117 · 1,840 packets ₹27,717 saved from the bin

Meet Munchly Foods

A mid-size snacks company in Pune, listed on the NSE, selling through 4 distributors and about 195 kirana shops in Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh and Telangana. Six products appear in this story; the first four are food, the last two are a personal-care line called Glowra. Prices are what a kirana customer pays (MRP) and what the distributor pays Munchly per packet, before GST.

The rule that drives everything: Blinkit's dark stores accept a batch only with 90 or more days to its best-before date; Zepto and Instamart want 60% of the shelf life still ahead, which for a 6-month product is 108 days. Stock that slips under those lines is sent back to the distributor, who has to sell it somewhere else or destroy it.

The carton at the centre of the story

In August, Rakesh Traders of Nagpur ordered 120 cartons of Masala Chips for the Ganesh-festival and Diwali season. Blinkit's Nagpur dark store took 43 cartons in September, then stopped accepting the batch: it had crossed the 90-day line. The remaining 77 cartons (76 full, one with 16 packets) sit on shelves B3 to B5 at Kalamna Market, selling to local shops at about 12 packets a day.

3Six doors09:21 · inside the agent
Every carton has six ways out of the warehouse. Five of them pay. The sixth sends a bill.

The agent prices every door, per carton, against the days left. The online marketplace pays about half of MRP and takes anything with 30 days or more. Local shops pay more but can only absorb what they will sell in two weeks. The brand's own site and a staff sale pay less. A food bank pays nothing but keeps the food as food. And the bin, as the table shows, is not free.

7The paperworkDay 3 · Anita's desk, finance, Pune
For the first time this year, a cleared batch arrives on Anita's desk as four finished documents instead of a question.

When stock is destroyed, the company must hand back the GST credit it claimed on it. When stock is sold, even cheaply, the credit stays. By day three Anita has, without asking: the tax invoice to Hyderabad, the credit note that pays Rakesh Traders for the free packets in the shop scheme, a note confirming no e-way bill was needed, and the memo that proves the credit was kept.

8The reportQuarter end · boardroom, Pune
Three months later the same 57 cartons appear again, this time as 218 kilos on a slide the auditor will sign.

Listed companies in India must now report their waste and have the number audited. Every rescued batch becomes a line in that report with evidence an auditor can follow: the invoice, the listing, the shop orders, the food-bank receipt. The 57 cartons of chips weigh 218 kilos with their packaging. Finance and sustainability read the same ledger; for the first time, the number for "stock we did not destroy" is a number.

5.7 tonnes kept out of landfill this quarter, with invoices behind every kilo. That goes straight into the annual report.Vikram

Munchly wrote off the stock, Anita's team paid ₹2,611 of GST credit back to the government, and nobody could say at year end how many tonnes the company had thrown away.
